# The Build-a-Graph Method

## Hook

An engineer who has already concluded, honestly, that a job needs a graph now faces a different problem: what to build first. Open a schema file too early and there's nothing yet to extract into it. Wire an audit loop before either graph holds a single claim and it has nothing to audit. This page names the order that keeps that from happening — six lettered stages, A through F, meant to be read as a build sequence rather than a menu of unrelated concerns.

## Explanation

Each stage below names the step where it gets a full worked treatment, by number and by the Part of the roadmap it lives in — use those as a pointer for browsing to the fuller material once you're ready to actually build that piece, rather than reading this page as a self-contained replacement for it. Treat this page as the map, not the territory.

**A. Confirm the job is graph-shaped before opening a schema file.** Not every tracking problem earns the cost of a schema, an extraction pass, and an ongoing resolution step. Step 16, over in Part 7 (Staying Grounded), walks through the handful of situations where a queue, a careful prompt, or a plain table does the job for less, and this course's fuller pre-build check — the [decision framework](decision-framework.md) — exists specifically to catch a "yes" that was really enthusiasm rather than an honest reading of the cost. Stage A is where that reading happens, before anything downstream gets built on top of a decision nobody actually tested.

**B. Pick which graph — or graphs — the job actually needs.** A tracking problem and a knowledge problem are not the same problem, and this course keeps them apart as the [two graphs](../02-foundations/the-two-graphs.md): a record of what's been tried and by whom, and a smaller, slower-growing record of what the team has actually verified. Plenty of real systems need both running side by side, connected only by a pointer that says "this confirmed claim came out of that attempt" rather than merged into one undifferentiated structure. Getting this choice wrong early — building only one when the job needed both, or blurring them into a single graph — is one of this course's recurring judgment failures, and it's cheapest to catch here, before either graph has any content in it.

**C. Design the schema before any extraction prompt gets written.** Entity types, relationship types, and what counts as a valid claim under this schema all need deciding on paper first, not discovered by whatever an extraction run happens to produce. Step 6, in Part 3 (The Graph of Facts), covers why that ordering matters and what a schema-first pass actually looks like against real source material.

**D. Wire the write path: extraction, then resolution, then provenance — and never in place.** New claims come in through extraction (Step 6), get checked against what the graph already holds through resolution (Step 7), and every one that survives that check gets a provenance record naming where it came from (Step 8) — all three still in Part 3. None of the three stages overwrites what came before it — a superseded claim gets marked superseded, a merged pair keeps a trail back to both originals, and the graph only ever grows a new version forward, never edits history out from under a reader who trusted an earlier one.

**E. Wire the read path: a bounded slice plus a checker that trusts edges over tone.** A worker pulling from the graph should never see the whole thing — Step 9, opening Part 4 (Working From the Graph), covers building a task-scoped subgraph sized to the job in front of it. Whatever that worker produces then gets checked against real graph edges rather than how confident the output sounds, which is what Step 10's grounded checker, right after it, is for.

**F. Add governance edges only once a specific failure has actually shown up.** Steps 11 through 13 name four separate ways a lone loop working against a shared graph can quietly fail itself — that trio sits under this course's fifth Part, on loops sharing a graph — and pairs each one with its own fix: something planted to catch a loop gaming its own scorecard, a second loop whose one job is checking the first, a rule for who wins when two loops disagree, and a fact no loop is ever allowed to touch. None of the four is free, and installing all of them on day one against failures that haven't happened yet is its own mistake, sized and named back in Part 7 at Step 17. Stage F only fires in response to evidence: a specific loop showing a specific one of those failure patterns, not a general sense that governance sounds responsible.

Read top to bottom the first time through; after that, treat each letter as a place to jump back to when the corresponding piece of a real build is what's actually in front of you.
